MABUHAY ANG FIESTA: VOICEOVER SCRIPT
====================================

HOW TO RECORD
1. Quiet, soft room (closet, curtains, bed). Phone about a hand-width from your mouth, a little to the side.
2. Stay silent for 5 seconds before you start.
3. Say the line number first, pause, then read the line. Example: "Line 1." (pause) "In a small barrio..."
   This lets me match every line to its scene. I will cut the numbers out.
4. Made a mistake? Pause, then say the WHOLE line again. I will keep the last clean take.
5. Leave about 1 second of silence between lines. Speak warmly, like telling a story to a child.
6. Words in quotes are the characters talking. You can change your voice a little for Lola Bining (old, gentle) and Tikoy (young, excited).
7. Send the original recording file (.m4a / .mp3 / .wav). Splitting it into 2-3 files is fine.

Estimated length: about 4.5 minutes.

--- PROLOGUE ---
Line 1. In a small barrio, the streets were strung with colourful flags. An old carabao named Lola Bining watched a little tarsier named Tikoy bounce with excitement.

Line 2. "Lola, tomorrow is the fiesta! But why does the whole town throw a party every single year?"

Line 3. Lola Bining smiled. "That party is older than this town, anak. Come, I will tell you how it began."

--- BEFORE 1521 ---
Line 4. Long before the Spanish came, the islands already had feasts. After a good harvest, whole communities gathered to give thanks.

Line 5. Ritual specialists called babaylan led offerings to the spirits, the anito and diwata, with rice, wine, music and dance.

Line 6. Spanish writers later described these gatherings, with gongs, chanting and shared food, sometimes lasting for days.

--- CEBU, 1521 ---
Line 7. In 1521, Ferdinand Magellan’s ships reached Cebu. Rajah Humabon and his people agreed to be baptised.

Line 8. As a gift, the queen received a small statue of the child Jesus. Filipinos call it the Santo Niño.

Line 9. Weeks later Magellan was killed at the Battle of Mactan by the forces of Lapulapu, and the Spaniards sailed away. But the little statue stayed.

--- CEBU, 1565 ---
Line 10. Forty-four years later, Miguel López de Legazpi arrived to set up a Spanish colony. During the fighting, part of the village burned.

Line 11. In the ashes of one house, a soldier found the Santo Niño, unharmed. The Spaniards took it as a miracle and built a church on the spot.

Line 12. Cebuanos honoured it with a prayer dance, two steps forward and one step back, like a river current. Today it is the Sinulog festival.

--- A SAINT FOR EVERY TOWN ---
Line 13. Over the next three centuries, the Spanish gathered scattered villages into towns built around a plaza and a church.

Line 14. Each town was given a patron saint. That saint’s feast day became the town’s biggest celebration of the year.

Line 15. Many feast days fell near planting or harvest, so the old thanksgiving feasts did not vanish. They blended into the new Catholic calendar.

--- THE FIESTA DAY ---
Line 16. A fiesta usually begins days before, with a novena: nine days of prayers in the church.

Line 17. On the feast day itself, there is a Mass and a procession. The saint’s image is carried through the streets on a decorated carriage, followed by people holding candles.

Line 18. Then the town comes alive. A brass band marches by, there is a fair with rides, a beauty pageant, a basketball tournament, and at night, fireworks.

--- BAHAY MO, BAHAY KO ---
Line 19. The heart of the fiesta is the open house. Families cook for days, and anyone who walks in is fed, even a stranger.

Line 20. Lechon, pancit, kare-kare, and sweet leche flan cover the table. Relatives working in Manila or abroad come home for it.

Line 21. Neighbours help each other cook, decorate and clean up. Filipinos call this spirit of working together bayanihan.

--- FAMOUS FIESTAS ---
Line 22. Every region adds its own colour. In Kalibo, Aklan, dancers paint their faces black for the Ati-Atihan, honouring the Santo Niño and, by local legend, the Ati people.

Line 23. In Lucban, Quezon, every May 15th, houses are covered in vegetables, fruit and leaf-shaped rice wafers called kiping for the Pahiyas, thanking San Isidro, patron of farmers.

Line 24. And some fiestas are brand new. Bacolod’s MassKara began in 1980, during a sugar crisis and a ferry tragedy, with smiling masks to lift the city’s spirits.

--- WHY IT LASTS ---
Line 25. Today almost every town and barangay in the Philippines holds at least one fiesta a year, and Filipino communities abroad hold their own, from Dubai to California.

Line 26. Not everyone loves every part of it. Some elders and priests warn against families going into debt just to feed the crowd.

Line 27. But at its core, the fiesta is the same thing it was before 1521: giving thanks, coming home, and sharing what you have with everyone.

--- EPILOGUE ---
Line 28. Tikoy’s big eyes grew even bigger. "So when we share food tomorrow, we are doing something our ancestors did?"

Line 29. "Exactly, anak," said Lola Bining. "Different saints, different songs, but the same thankful heart."

Line 30. That night the sky burst with fireworks, the band played, and the whole barrio ate together. Mabuhay ang fiesta!
