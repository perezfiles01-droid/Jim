THE ASWANG: WHERE THE LEGEND COMES FROM. VOICEOVER SCRIPT (DRAFT FOR APPROVAL)
==============================================================================
Narrator: Kokoro af_heart (American female), speed 0.92
Characters: Lola Bining (old carabao) and Tikoy (young tarsier), reused
Estimated length: about 4.5 minutes, 9 scenes, 30 lines

--- PROLOGUE ---
Line 1. On a quiet night in the barrio, the frogs were singing and the bamboo creaked in the wind. Little Tikoy pulled his blanket up to his big round eyes.
Line 2. "Lola Bining, my cousin says an aswang lives in the next village. Is it true? What is an aswang?"
Line 3. Lola Bining chuckled softly. "That story is more than four hundred years old, anak. Come closer, and I will tell you where it began."

--- WHAT IS AN ASWANG? ---
Line 4. In Philippine folklore, aswang is a name for many night creatures at once. It can mean a shapeshifter, a witch, a ghoul, or a vampire.
Line 5. By day, the story goes, an aswang looks like an ordinary neighbour. By night, it may turn into a black dog, a pig, or a large bird.
Line 6. One famous kind, the manananggal, leaves the bottom half of its body behind and flies away on bat wings.

--- 1582 AND 1589 ---
Line 7. The oldest written records come from Spanish colonial writers. In 1582, Miguel de Loarca described a Visayan belief in a creature he wrote as asuang.
Line 8. In 1589, the friar Juan de Plasencia listed the osuang among Tagalog beliefs: someone said to fly at night and eat human flesh.
Line 9. Some friars used these stories to paint the old healers, the babaylan, as witches. Many of the women who led rituals were treated as dangerous.

--- THE VILLAGE AT NIGHT ---
Line 10. For centuries, the tales passed from grandparent to grandchild, around oil lamps and under nipa roofs.
Line 11. People said a tiktik bird calls softly when an aswang is near, and that the softer the call, the closer it really is.
Line 12. To stay safe, families hung garlic, kept salt and vinegar near the door, and carried a buntot pagi, the tail of a stingray.

--- CAPIZ ---
Line 13. Over time, one province became tied to the legend more than any other: Capiz, on the island of Panay.
Line 14. Researchers think trade, rivalry and gossip between regions helped give Capiz that reputation. People there have long found it unfair.
Line 15. Today Capiz is better known for its seafood and its shining capiz shell windows, but the old joke still follows it.

--- 1950s: A WAR STORY ---
Line 16. The legend even reached a real war. In the early nineteen fifties, the government fought the Huk rebels in central Luzon.
Line 17. American officer Edward Lansdale later wrote that his team spread aswang rumours near a rebel camp, then staged a body to look like a victim.
Line 18. He claimed the frightened rebels left the area. Historians still debate how much of his story happened exactly as told.

--- WHAT SCHOLARS SAY ---
Line 19. In the nineteen sixties and seventies, folklorist Maximo Ramos collected aswang stories from all over the islands.
Line 20. He found that the aswang often explained frightening things: a sudden illness, a baby lost before birth, a death no one understood.
Line 21. Scholars such as Herminia Meñez saw another pattern. The person called aswang was often an outsider, a loner, or someone who was simply different.
Line 22. No scientist has ever found a real aswang. But the fear it caused was very real, and sometimes innocent people were blamed.

--- WHY THE STORY LIVES ON ---
Line 23. Today the aswang appears in films, comics and games, and it has become one of the best known creatures of Filipino storytelling.
Line 24. Its stories also kept old knowledge alive: names of plants, rules about the night, and the habit of looking after one another.
Line 25. "So the aswang is a story that tells us what people were afraid of," said Tikoy slowly.

--- EPILOGUE ---
Line 26. "Exactly, anak," said Lola Bining. "And a good lesson is never to call someone a monster just because they are different."
Line 27. Outside, a small bird called from the mango tree. Tikoy jumped.
Line 28. Lola Bining laughed. "That is only a kuwago, an owl, looking for its supper."
Line 29. Tikoy giggled, snuggled close, and finally closed his big round eyes.
Line 30. And in the quiet barrio, the stories kept on whispering, just as they have for four hundred years.
